We are working with Jonathan Lessard, a professor at Concordia University, on a game concept for the CART 415 Winter studio. The eventual game is intended for an end-of-year exhibition.

**The project brief**
The goal is a simple, persistent, asynchronous multiplayer game that people can enter and leave without disrupting play. At the exhibition, visitors would scan a QR code, use their phones as controllers, and see their avatars on a shared projected screen. The game should work with roughly 20–30 players, but also remain playable with just one.
t is to research ideas, prototype them, identify design and technical risks, and give next semester’s CART 415 students a playable starting point and a clear development foundation. Lessard is interested in both the game concept and how a large team could realistically build it within a limited timeline.

**How the team developed its ideas**
The team began by researching game references, phone-based multiplayer, objectives and feedback, visual communication, and technical feasibility. Members brainstormed concepts individually, then grouped them into cooperative, team-based, and individual-play approaches.

They narrowed the options to three concepts:

Tower Game — a construction/Jenga-style tower concept.
Soccer Game — a free-for-all soccer concept.
Maze Mosaic — a maze-based concept built around a mosaic.
They compared these ideas against the project’s needs: multiplayer support, asynchronous play, simplicity, and ways to motivate players. For the client presentation, they prepared gameplay descriptions, visual concepts, Unity demonstrations, and a prototype phone-controller layout with directional and action buttons.

**Main challenges**
The biggest design challenge is making the game engaging while keeping it feasible to produce. In particular, the team identified four issues: supporting players who join or leave at any time, keeping the rules easy to understand, making the projected screen readable for a crowd, and giving players a reason to keep participating.

**Decision and next steps**
After seeing the three concepts, Lessard selected Maze Mosaic as the foundation for further development. The team and client then discussed adjustments and new directions, calling this refinement Maze Mosaic 2.0. The presentation does not specify all of its final rules or mechanics.

The next phase has two priorities: establishing the game’s artistic direction and visual style, and refining and testing its mechanics and player interactions. The overall aim is a visually clear, playable prototype that demonstrates the concept’s gameplay and technical feasibility for next semester’s team.
